# OCEAN63-P2　Juvenile growth, parental feeding and selection in a simulated aquatic habitat

22 November 2026. In P1, newborn individuals consumed older ones, including a newborn's own parent. The author proposed that offspring start small, grow at their own expense and arrive in litters of three. Close relatives are protected from live predation, and juveniles can draw energy from their parent on contact. P2 tests whether predation becomes more frequent over generations when food particles are scarce and whether early hunters leave more surviving offspring. We also measure turns toward particles and live prey and turns away from potential predators.

## Population and environment

The native threemind.continuous-memory/6.3 Core uses single-parent threemind.birth/2. Offspring inherit nodes, connections, persistent state, decay ages and peripheral mappings. They start with cleared activity and a new internal clock. The environmental rules have their own identity, threemind.ocean-juvenile-kin-care/1. Two independent habitat seeds (471101 and 471102, also used in P1) each start with 53 blank founders and 53 food particles. For each seed we compare one new particle every 1 or 8 simulated seconds. We verify paired initial bodies, Core states, peripherals, energy accounts and random states. Follow each world to 2,400 simulated seconds or extinction and record its state at 600, 900, 1,500 and 2,400 seconds. The two initial habitats are the independent units.

Founders start with a radius of 4 mm. Offspring start at at most 4 mm and at most half their parent's current radius. Adult target size is inherited with the existing body-trait mutation rules. Once a second an individual can spend energy above its neonatal body reserve on growth, up to 1/120 of the construction cost of its adult body per second. Half of this expenditure becomes edible body material and half dissipates as heat. Each individual starts with one particle's worth of Core energy (342.375 units). Its initial body reserve is the same amount multiplied by the cube of its birth radius divided by 8 mm, giving 42.797 units at 4 mm.

The body supplies the Core from its own reserves. As paid body volume increases from birth to adult volume, the target Core reserve rises linearly from one particle's energy to four, matching the adult target in P1.

An active parent can reproduce once it is at least 60 seconds old, has reached 75% of its own adult target radius and has gone at least 60 seconds since its previous litter. It pays for three offspring at once, including their Core energy, body reserves, construction and a reproductive cost of 171.187 units each. The parent retains at least 684.749 units. Newborns are placed 120 degrees apart and clear of their parent and siblings. Capacity limits are 97 living individuals and 1,024 births. Defer the entire litter when fewer than three places remain.

Every 20 simulated milliseconds we check physical contact. Each individual's lineage includes itself and its three most recent ancestors. Individuals with intersecting lineages are protected from live predation. Among other individuals the larger one consumes the smaller on contact. Equal-sized individuals collide. Naturally dead bodies remain available to scavengers. Shell handling can make a meal a net energy loss. After live predation is resolved, an offspring under 30 seconds old in contact with its surviving direct parent may receive up to one energy unit per second (0.02 per contact sample). Simultaneous requests share the parent's available surplus proportionally, limited by the recipient's available space and the parent's 684.749-unit reserve floor. Energy credited to offspring is debited from the parent. Kinship labels and correct actions are unavailable to the Core.

## Outcomes

1. Compare live kills per 100 living-body minutes over each individual's first 180 seconds between generations 1–2 and 3–5. An increase in both sparse habitats supports a generational rise. A greater increase under sparse supply in both pairs supports an additional effect of reducing food supply.
2. Classify nonfounders surviving to age 60 by hunting during their first 60 seconds. Count children born when these individuals are aged 60 to 180 seconds who themselves reach age 60. Include zero-offspring parents and apply P1's common follow-up rules. Compare within generation and birth-size strata. More surviving children of early hunters in both sparse habitats supports a reproductive advantage. Also count living descendants at parental age 240. Report unavailable cohorts or comparison groups.
3. Apply the P1 kinematic criteria for turns toward particles, turns that precede live predation, and evasive turns by an individual whose original course would have brought it into contact with a larger one. Count approaches among eligible live pairs using size at approach. Report opportunities, qualifying events, individuals and times. An observation recurs across habitats if it occurs in both independent habitats.
4. Record litter bills, neonatal sizes, generations and survival, nursing contact duration, actual transfers and recipients, scavenging and energy spent on growth. Kin protection is imposed by the habitat. Actual movements and selective advantages are empirical outcomes. The paired comparison within P2 tests food supply; the change from P1 to P2 includes several environmental changes.
